About

Researching the relationship between place, history, systems, and health.

Dr. Leia D. Belt is an interdisciplinary sociologist and population health scholar whose work investigates how historical systems of exclusion continue to influence contemporary communities, institutions, environments, and health. Her research moves between historical and contemporary evidence, and across methodological traditions — treating a research question, not a discipline's default toolkit, as the thing that determines method.

That range shows up in the evidence she works with: population health data and administrative records sit alongside oral histories, interview transcripts, PhotoVoice images, archival documents, and geographic and spatial data. She is as comfortable in a historical archive as she is with a multilevel regression model — and treats both as necessary for understanding how structural conditions become embodied, generation after generation, in specific places.

Throughout, she is concerned with research that is not only methodologically rigorous but meaningful to the communities represented in the data — work developed with communities, not simply conducted on them.

Training & pathway. Dr. Belt holds a Ph.D. in Interdisciplinary Studies — a designed emphasis in Sociology, History, and Public Health — from the University of Iowa (2023), an M.A. in Sociology from the University of Iowa (2020), and a B.S. in Psychology and Sociology, with honors, from Kent State University (2017), where she was a Ronald E. McNair Scholar. Her dissertation, "Sundown Towns and Contemporary Maternal Infant Health: An Interdisciplinary Mixed-Methods Analysis of Structural Racism and Its Public Health Legacy in the Midwest," laid the foundation for her ongoing sundown-towns research program.

Fellowships & training. She is a Health Affairs Equity Fellow (2024–2025), an AcademyHealth Health Data Policy Fellow (2024), and a graduate of the National Endowment for the Humanities' Born-Digital Scholarly Publishing Institute at Brown University Library (2024) — training that directly informs the born-digital book project below.

Postdoctoral research. Before her current appointment, she was a Postdoctoral Scholar in the Department of Sociology and the Higher Education Race & The Economy (HERE) Lab at UC Merced (2023–2025).

Research Philosophy

“

Health happens somewhere.

Health is shaped not only by individual behavior or healthcare access but by the histories, policies, institutions, economies, environments, and relationships embedded in place. Understanding contemporary health therefore requires asking how communities came to look the way they do — and who had the power to determine that development.

01

History

Investigate the policies, institutions, racial hierarchies, and historical events that produced contemporary places.

02

Place

Examine how those histories become embedded in neighborhoods, towns, rural communities, infrastructure, environments, and access to resources.

03

Health

Study how those structural conditions shape population health, maternal and infant health, well-being, and access to care.

04

Community

Combine population-level evidence with the experiences, knowledge, memories, and priorities of people living within those places.

The Geography of Exclusion

Mapping the Afterlife of Sundown Towns

This body of scholarship investigates how historically racially exclusionary places — sundown towns and similar jurisdictions that used law, custom, threat, and violence to exclude Black residents — may continue to structure health, opportunity, resources, community life, and inequality today. The research question is not only historical: it asks how that exclusion persists through contemporary systems, and what that persistence means for the people living in and near those places now.

Map the History

Locating sundown towns and exclusionary jurisdictions using historical records, newspapers, and geographic databases.

Measure the Present

Linking that historical geography to contemporary population health and demographic outcomes.

Listen to Communities

Gathering oral histories and community knowledge from people who lived this geography.

Trace the Mechanisms

Identifying the transportation corridors, institutions, and policies that carried exclusion forward.

Build the Archive

Assembling historical documents, photographs, and records into a lasting, shareable public resource.

Community-Engaged Public Health Data

Who should shape the data, not just who should collect it.

In California's San Joaquin Valley and Sierra Foothills, Dr. Belt works on community-engaged public health data infrastructure — asking not only "what data should we collect?" but also "who should shape the data, interpret it, trust it, and ultimately own how it is used?"

This work sits at the intersection of public-facing health dashboards, community crowdsourcing, resource sharing, warm handoffs between services, community stewardship, institutional trust, co-design, and data sovereignty.

  • Public-facing health dashboards, built with community input
